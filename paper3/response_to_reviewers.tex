\section*{To Professor Cepin and the Reviewers}

We thank both reviewers for reports that were unusually specific and, in two places, correct about
problems we had not seen. The revision is substantial: one comment invalidated the calibration
scheme behind every number in Section~5, and we re-ran the entire experimental campaign rather than
patch around it. All twelve comments are addressed below, and five changes are worth flagging
first.

\textbf{1. The calibration was not i.i.d., and every number has been recomputed.} Reviewer~1's
comment~5 was correct. Scores stacked at three monitoring points per unit
correlate at $\rho=0.908$, a design effect of 2.82, so the published calibration set of 1200 scores
carried the information of roughly 426 independent ones. The primary analysis is now unit-level,
one score per unit. The headline survives: regime-blind conformal miscovers by 0.132 and SCC
recovers coverage to within 0.011 of target, against 0.006 previously, and the change is what
theory predicts, since losing a factor of three in stacking should inflate the finite-sample floor
by $\sqrt3=1.73$, giving $0.006\times1.73=0.010$ against 0.011 observed.

\textbf{2. A second dataset, with the positive and the negative both reported.} On NASA C-MAPSS
FD002 and FD004, 509 run-to-failure engines across six operating regimes, the coverage gap falls
from 0.590 to 0.060 and from 0.567 to 0.058. The a-priori certificate, however, does not transfer:
neither physically motivated departure quantity predicts the residual gap, and the bound holds on
67 to 83\% of held-out regime pairs against 100\% on the testbed. We report this as the boundary of
the contribution rather than omitting it.

\textbf{3. The certificate claim is narrowed accordingly.} The abstract and conclusions now claim
the a-priori bound only where the governing degradation law identifies the departure quantity
$\boldsymbol\psi$, as ISO~281 does for rolling-contact fatigue and no comparable standard does for
a turbofan gas path. The dimensionless calibration, which is the part a practitioner deploys, is
claimed broadly and is now validated on external data.

\textbf{4. The actionability limit is conceded, not argued.} At $\eta=2$ the interval back-off is
1.011, so the certified lower bound is non-positive on average. The word ``graceful'' has been
removed from the manuscript and replaced by a stated operating envelope.

\textbf{5. Two errors we found ourselves during the revision, both now fixed.} The robustness table
had been computed under the old stacked calibration, which would have left two calibration schemes
side by side in one paper; it has been re-run unit-level. Its ``held-out'' bound-satisfaction rate
was in fact computed in sample; it is now a genuine 60/40 split, as the caption always claimed.

Every number in the revised manuscript is reproduced by a committed script in the public repository,
including the figures, which previously had no generator. The C-MAPSS files were verified against
the NASA release by checksum: \texttt{train\_FD002.txt} has MD5\\
\texttt{b6eaab2a6b589e5e41d43ca2f99e379b}.

\section*{Reviewer 1}

\subsection*{Comment 1: dependence on base-model complexity}
\begin{comment}
Although the authors claim that the proposed method is applicable to any pre-trained predictor as a
``drop-in modification,'' the case study only tests extrapolation using an extremely simple nominal
rate model. If a highly non-linear machine learning model (such as Random Forest or a Neural
Network) were adopted as the base predictor, interpolation and extrapolation errors across
operating regimes could become complicated. Consequently, there is a concern that the proposed
dimensionless scale $\sigma(\theta)$ alone might not fully absorb the shift in the score
distribution. The authors should evaluate how the conservatism of the a-priori bound changes when
varying the complexity of the base model.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} We ran the experiment with four predictors of increasing capacity, each
trained on the source condition only and deployed on the target, and we answer both halves of the
comment: what happens to coverage, and what happens to the conservatism of the bound.

\emph{Coverage.} The concern is well founded, and the damage falls entirely on the unscaled
baseline. The naive coverage gap at $\eta=0$ worsens by a factor of 3.5, from 0.127 for the
closed-form extrapolator to 0.449 for a random forest, because a learner fitted flexibly to one
operating regime fails harder when deployed on another. SCC is flat at 0.007 to 0.014 across all
four classes, and under departure the more flexible learners are the better ones, 0.046 for a
neural network against 0.102 for the closed form at $\eta=2$, because a model fitted in the
dimensionless coordinates absorbs part of the residual departure.

\emph{Conservatism of the bound.} We fitted the certificate separately for each predictor class over
the same configurations. Validity is unaffected: the bound holds on 100\% of held-out configurations
for all four, with margins in the narrow band 0.30 to 0.36, so its conservatism does not grow with
model capacity. Its mechanism, however, does weaken, and we state that rather than leave it for a
reader to find. The departure model explains the score-distribution mismatch well only for the
closed-form predictor (held-out $R^2=0.90$, against $-2.8$ to $-0.02$ for the three learned ones),
so with a learned predictor the bound holds on conservatism rather than because $\delta$ tracks the
mismatch: the residual is then dominated by model error rather than by physical departure. This is
the same pattern the C-MAPSS certificate shows in the reply to Reviewer~2's major comment~1, and a
further reason the certificate claim has been narrowed in this revision.

We also state the caveat the result carries: the drop-in property holds provided the learned
predictor is fitted in the dimensionless coordinates, since no conformal layer can repair a point
predictor that does not transfer.

We note one further finding rather than hide it. On C-MAPSS the ordering of the naive column runs
the other way, because the raw sensor levels there encode the operating regime itself, so a flexible
learner can partly infer the regime and compensate. What is invariant across both datasets is that
SCC is the better arm in every row.

predictor class, and two new columns in Table~2.}

\subsection*{Comment 2: deployability and the data environment the method needs}
\begin{comment}
In the FEMTO dataset, the runtime diagnostic returns an ``indeterminate'' status and safely falls
back, which the authors positively evaluate as an ``honest applicability limit.'' However, in the
practical field of reliability engineering (PHM), if even a widely and commonly used benchmark
dataset is rejected due to ``data scarcity and variability,'' the cases where this method can be
truly considered ``deployable'' might be extremely limited. The authors should quantitatively
clarify the practical requirements (design guidelines) regarding what kind of data environment
(e.g., minimum required number of units or signal-to-noise ratio) is necessary to yield a ``holds''
status.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} We have mapped the requirement on exactly the two axes the comment
names: units per condition, and the unit-to-unit life scatter, which is the signal-to-noise ratio
for this test, since the structural signal is the L10 life ratio between conditions and the noise is
the spread within a condition.

The requirement is modest. Three units per condition suffice at a scatter of 0.10, six at 0.25 and
ten at 0.50; only at a scatter of 0.80 does it rise to about forty units. The C-MAPSS fleet added in
this revision, at roughly 260 units per regime, sits far inside the feasible region, so the method
is not confined to unusually rich datasets.

The map also settles the status of the FEMTO result. With six bearings per condition and a
sevenfold life spread, that dataset sits in the worst corner of the map, where \emph{holds} is
returned in 0.50 to 0.00 of trials, so its \emph{indeterminate} verdict is predicted by the data
environment rather than being a quirk of the dataset.

We also report a property of our own test that the map makes visible: since the reported verdict is
the worst of three pairwise interval tests at a nominal 95\% each, the achievable rate under exact
similitude is about $0.95^3\approx0.86$, which is where the larger fleets settle. A
multiplicity-corrected verdict would raise that ceiling and is the obvious refinement; we have left
the test as submitted and stated the ceiling rather than redesign the diagnostic during revision.

\where{New opening and Table~6 in Section~5.8, with the FEMTO paragraph now referring back to the
map.}

\subsection*{Comment 4: what happens when the assumed physics is wrong}
\begin{comment}
The proposed method assumes that accurate physical parameters (e.g., load, speed, temperature) and
the governing equations are known a priori through the Buckingham Pi theorem. In actual complex
industrial machinery, however, the governing equations for degradation (such as Arrhenius or
Lundberg-Palmgren) are not always known. A sensitivity analysis or a theoretical discussion is
required regarding the risk that the dimensionless correction could backfire (i.e., amplify the
data shift) if the physical model is partially misspecified or if unknown environmental factors are
present.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} The comment names two distinct risks and we now treat them separately.

\emph{Unknown environmental factors} are the case the departure sweep already covers: the secondary
Arrhenius channel of weight $\eta$ is an unmodelled physical effect the scale does not know about,
and Section~5.2 reports what it costs, together with the operating envelope requested by
Reviewer~2's major comment~4.

\emph{Partial misspecification of the governing law} is new work. We perturb the assumed activation
energy while holding $k(T_{\mathrm{ref}})$ fixed, so only the between-condition behaviour of the
scale is distorted, which is the part similitude depends on. When only the scale is wrong and the
predictor is unaffected, the SCC gap stays at or below 0.05 out to $\lvert\Delta E/E\rvert=31\%$,
remains better than not scaling at all out to $+62\%$, and backfires, in the reviewer's sense of
being worse than the unscaled baseline, only at $+100\%$ (0.139 against 0.132). When a single wrong
model drives both the predictor and the scale, which is the realistic case, the method is much more
fragile and backfires already at $-10\%$ (0.072 against 0.061).

The failure is not silent. The runtime invariance check returns \emph{holds} only at the correct
scale ($g=0.967$, interval $[0.92,1.01]$) and \emph{violated} at every misspecification tested,
including the $-10\%$ point at which the coupled case first turns against the method ($g=1.215$,
$[1.16,1.27]$). This is a capability the submitted manuscript did not claim: because the check
tests the invariance of the dimensionless life, it is sensitive to misspecification of the scale
itself as well as to a departure from similitude.

\where{New Section~5.7 with Table~5; the dual role is now stated at the end of Section~3.6.}

\subsection*{Comment 5: within-unit dependence in the calibration set}
\begin{comment}
When applying conformal prediction to time-series or degradation data like RUL, strong temporal
correlations (dependencies) exist in data continuously acquired from the same unit (history between
time steps). Strictly speaking, this causes the assumptions of i.i.d. or exchangeability to waver
even within the same operating regime. While the proposed method addresses cross-regime shifts
among different units (spatial shifts) using a physical scale, the authors should explain the
theoretical consistency and the impact of temporal dependencies within the same unit on the
validity of the conformal prediction layer (e.g., whether block conformal approaches are
necessary).
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} The reviewer is right, and this was the most consequential comment in
either report. The submitted calibration stacked one score per (unit, monitoring fraction), so each
unit contributed three strongly dependent points. We measured the dependence rather than argue about
it: pairwise correlations across monitoring fractions of 0.965, 0.883 and 0.877, mean $\rho=0.908$,
design effect $1+(F-1)\rho=2.82$, so the published $n=1200$ carried an effective $n$ of about 426
against 400 units.

The primary analysis is now unit-level, one score per unit, and every number and figure in Section~5
has been recomputed on that basis. The finding is unchanged: naive conformal miscovers by 0.132
while SCC recovers to 0.011. The change in the floor, from 0.006 to 0.011, is what theory predicts
for the loss of a factor of three in stacking, $\sqrt3\times0.006=0.010$.

We also report the stronger option the comment implies. Where a guarantee is wanted simultaneously
at every monitoring point of a unit rather than at one sampled point, block conformal calibration on
the per-unit maximum score is exchangeable across units by construction; it costs conservatism, a
gap of 0.007 at $\eta=0$ and 0.173 at $\eta=2$ against 0.011 and 0.097 for single-point unit-level
calibration. We report it as the option for multi-point monitoring and state explicitly that
exchangeability is required across \emph{units}, not across time steps within a unit.

\where{New Section~3.4; the calibration scheme and the averaging convention are stated in
Section~4.3; all of Section~5, Table~1 and Figures~2 to 4 recomputed unit-level.}

\section*{Reviewer 2}

\subsection*{Major comment 1: no dataset the authors did not design}
\begin{comment}
The entire empirical demonstration of the coverage guarantee in Eq.~(1) uses a single simulator
(Section 4.2, the first-order Arrhenius catalyst-deactivation ODE) in which the similitude-departure
parameter $\psi$ is literally the knob $\eta$ that the author sets. Sections 5.1 through 5.4 and
Table 1 all report results from this one system. FEMTO (Section 5.5) only tests the diagnostic,
which correctly declines to certify, so it never exercises the certificate itself. That means there
is no case in the paper where the coverage bound is checked against a physical asset whose true
departure was not authored by the same person running the estimator. You could validate the
certificate itself, not just the diagnostic, on a second physically grounded dataset with enough pe
condition replication to establish ground truth. For instance, a bearing fatigue dataset with
multiple load and speed conditions and enough units per condition that the L10 comparison in
Section 3.5 is well powered rather than indeterminate as it was for FEMTO's six bearings per
condition.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} We accepted this and added a second dataset. On the choice of dataset:
we looked first for the bearing fleet the comment suggests, and to our knowledge no public
run-to-failure bearing dataset provides the replication the L10 comparison needs. The standard sets
give roughly five to six units per operating condition, an order of magnitude short of the
requirement mapped in Section~5.8 at the life scatter those datasets exhibit, and that shortfall is
what produced FEMTO's \emph{indeterminate} verdict in the first place, so a comparable bearing set
would reproduce the same non-result. We therefore went to NASA C-MAPSS, FD002 (260 engines) and
FD004 (249), six operating regimes with every engine visiting all of them, which gives roughly 260
units per regime, about $43\times$ FEMTO's replication, and which we did not author.

The dimensionless coordinate there is derived rather than fitted, from referred (corrected)
gas-turbine parameters, and it is self-checking: the sea-level static regime returns
$\theta=1.0000$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{p}}=0.9999$, and the implied damage clock spans $3.5\times$
across the six regimes.

\emph{The calibration transfers.} Regime-blind conformal loses a mean 0.590 of coverage across the
30 ordered regime pairs of FD002; referring the sensors to standard day is not enough on its own and
still loses 0.550; calibrating in the gas-path deviation coordinate recovers coverage to within
0.060 of the 0.90 target, a $9.8\times$ reduction, and FD004 replicates it at 0.567 to 0.058. No
target failure data is used. We also checked that this is not an artefact of a weak base predictor:
with a random forest on raw sensors the naive gap is 0.128 rather than 0.590, and SCC still improves
on it at 0.053.

\emph{The certificate does not transfer, and we report that.} Neither departure candidate identifies
the residual gap: the ambient referred distance correlates 0.289 with it on FD002 and
0.179 on FD004, and the healthy gas-path signature distance correlates $-0.122$ and $-0.086$; held
out, both regressions explain nothing, with $R^2$ between $-0.074$ and 0.022. The bound consequently
holds on 67 to 83\% of held-out regime pairs against 100\% on the testbed. We therefore separate the
two halves of the contribution in the abstract, in Section~5.5 and in the conclusions: the
dimensionless calibration is claimed broadly and is now validated externally; the a-priori
certificate is claimed only where the governing law identifies $\boldsymbol\psi$.

\where{New Section~4.4 (design and protocol), new Section~5.5 (results, both halves), Table~3,
Figure~2(b), and the narrowed claim in the abstract and Section~7.}

\subsection*{Major comment 2: $\boldsymbol\psi$ is never made concrete}
\begin{comment}
Section 3.2 sketches how the dimensionless scale $\sigma$ and departure $\psi$ arise conceptually
for two textbook cases, rolling-contact fatigue and Arrhenius kinetics, but $\psi$ is never
actually derived for either one. Only the simulator's $\eta$ stands in for $\psi$ anywhere in the
paper. A practitioner reading this paper and trying to apply SCC to their own asset has no worked
example of identifying which Buckingham $\Pi$ groups become the scale and which become the residual
departure outside the one system the author designed. Please work through the Lundberg-Palmgren
case in an appendix, derive the actual dimensionless groups from the rating-life relation, state
explicitly what physical effect constitutes $\psi$ for that model, and show at least one numerical
instance of computing it from published bearing parameters.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} A new appendix works the split through for rolling-contact fatigue,
the best-standardised degradation law in the reliability literature. Dimensional analysis on life,
load, dynamic capacity and speed gives two groups, $\Pi_1=tn$ and $\Pi_2=P/C$, and
Lundberg--Palmgren is precisely the relation between them, so the scale is
$\sigma=10^{6}(C/P)^{p}/(60n)$ in hours. ISO~281 then identifies what is left over: the modified
life $L_{nm}=a_1a_{\mathrm{ISO}}L_{10}$ has $a_{\mathrm{ISO}}=f(e_CC_u/P,\kappa)$, so for this law
the standard itself enumerates $\boldsymbol\psi$ as the lubrication regime and the contamination
group.

Evaluated on the FEMTO conditions, the appendix reproduces the $1.63\times$ characteristic-life
ratio from first principles, and it yields an instruction an engineer would not guess from the
rating-life formula: at a matched thermal state the residual departure is 0.091, while a
25\,$^\circ$C spread in operating temperature raises it to 1.060, roughly $12\times$ larger. For
bearings it is the thermal and lubrication state, not the load, that breaks similitude.

This also connects to major comment~1. Where the governing standard enumerates the residual groups,
$\boldsymbol\psi$ is identifiable and the a-priori bound applies; no equivalent enumeration exists
for a turbofan gas path, which is the most plausible reading of the C-MAPSS negative. Identifiability
of $\boldsymbol\psi$, rather than the bound itself, is the practical boundary of the method.

\where{New Appendix~B with Tables~B.1 and B.2, cross-referenced from Sections~3.2, 5.5 and 5.8.}

\subsection*{Major comment 3: comparison against discrete-mode conformal}
\begin{comment}
Section 2 discusses Javanmardi and Hüllermeier [5] as the closest prior treatment of conformalized
RUL, noting that their fix for varying operating conditions is to cluster into discrete modes. That
comparison stays qualitative. Section 5's case study never runs the clustering approach as a third
baseline alongside naïve and SCC conformal in Fig. 2. Since [5] is presented as the direct
predecessor this paper improves on. Please add a discretized-temperature clustering baseline
(treating the three testbed temperatures as [5]'s discrete modes) to Fig. 2, so the reader sees
quantitatively what continuous physical scaling buys over discrete mode clustering rather than
taking the qualitative contrast on faith.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} Added, with the most favourable reading given to the baseline: the
three testbed temperatures are supplied to it as known modes. The comparison is honest in both
directions.

When the target condition has its own run-to-failure history, discrete-mode calibration is
near-oracle and, once the departure is large, better than SCC (0.009 against 0.045 at $\eta=1$). We
state plainly that in that situation the discrete remedy is the right tool and the physical scale is
not needed.

When the target condition has no failure history, the discrete method has no calibration set for
that mode, falls back to the nearest mode, and lands close to regime-blind pooling, 0.078 to 0.138
against 0.093 to 0.159 pooled, while SCC holds 0.009 to 0.040, a gap smaller by $3.5\times$ to
$8.7\times$. That regime, a newly commissioned unit or a new operating point, is the one SCC exists
for.

fallback inherits the same directional failure as regime-blind calibration.}

\subsection*{Major comment 4: are the intervals actionable at large departures}
\begin{comment}
Section 4.3 defines efficiency such that a diverging value ``signals degenerate, uselessly wide
intervals,'' and Section 5.2 reports that at $\eta = 2$ the relative back-off reaches 1.01
(Fig. 3), meaning the certified interval half-width is roughly the size of the point RUL estimate
itself. That same section describes this outcome as the gap and back-off degrading ``gracefully.''
In my understanding, those two statements are in tension: by the paper's own definition of
degeneracy, a back-off near 1.0 is close to the line the paper itself says marks an uninformative
interval. Could you please state explicitly, using the paper's own efficiency criterion, what
back-off value should be read as the point where SCC's output stops being actionable for a
maintenance planner, and say plainly whether the $\eta = 2$ configuration in Fig. 3 is on the safe
or the degenerate side of that line?
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} The two statements were in tension, and the reviewer is right about
which one has to go. We answer the two questions plainly.

\emph{Where actionability stops.} Efficiency is now defined as the relative back-off
$b=q_T/\overline{\mathrm{RUL}}_T$, and because SCC returns a one-sided lower bound, the quantity a
planner acts on is the certified fraction of predicted life, $1-b$. Three bands are fixed in
Section~4.3: $b<0.5$ is actionable, since more than half the predicted life is certified;
$0.5\le b<1$ is degraded, a positive but shrinking lead time; and $b\ge1$ is degenerate, because the
certified lower bound is then non-positive on average, so the interval states only that failure has
not yet occurred and carries no replacement schedule.

\emph{Which side $\eta=2$ is on.} The degenerate side. The measured back-off there is 1.011 and the
certified fraction of predicted life is $-0.011$. Coverage is still certified, with a gap of 0.097,
and Section~5.2 says so, but the interval is not actionable. The envelope is now stated in full: SCC
is actionable out to $\eta=0.68$, degraded to $\eta=1.97$, and degenerate beyond, with the band
edges drawn on the figure. The word ``graceful'' has been removed from the manuscript entirely, in
the abstract, Section~4.3, Section~6 and Section~7 as well as Section~5.2.

\where{Efficiency and the bands defined in Section~4.3; the envelope stated in Section~5.2; bands
shaded in Figure~3; ``graceful'' removed from the abstract, Section~4.3, Section~6 and Section~7.}

\subsection*{Minor comment 1: the dimension of $\boldsymbol\psi$ and the choice of norm}
\begin{comment}
Definition 1 (Appendix A) defines $\psi$ as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$, but every case-study
configuration varies a single scalar departure via $\eta$. Please state explicitly that the tested
case is $m = 1$, and note whether the norm choice in Eq. (1) matters once $m > 1$ is actually
exercised.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} Correct, and now stated. The dimension $m$ is set by the physics rather
than by the method, and the case exercised in this paper has $m=1$, a scalar weight on a single
unmodelled channel, so the norm reduces to an absolute value and its choice is immaterial. For
$m>1$ the norm must be fixed at the same time as $\boldsymbol\psi$, since $L$ is estimated in that
norm and rescales with it. Appendix~B gives the concrete two-dimensional case: carrying the
contamination group alongside the lubrication regime makes $\boldsymbol\psi$ two-dimensional, and we
say why the appendix uses the scalar $\ln\kappa$ instead.

\subsection*{Minor comment 2: the bound holds on 100\% of configurations, which suggests looseness}
\begin{comment}
Table 1 reports Bound \% = 100 in every row. A bound that always holds could simply be loose
everywhere rather than informative. You could report the average or worst-case gap between the
bound and the measured coverage gap so a reader can judge tightness, not just validity.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} The suspicion is correct and we now report it. Across held-out
configurations the mean certified bound is 0.384 against a mean measured gap of 0.046, a margin of
0.339 with a range of 0.268 to 0.552, so the certificate is conservative by roughly a factor of
eight. We also name the two sources. The dominant one is the intercept rather than the physical
slope, since at $\delta=0$ the bound is $2a=0.278$ against a measured gap of 0.011; that intercept
is the finite-sample floor of the plug-in total-variation estimator and decays as $n^{-1/2}$ in the
number of calibration units, from 0.424 at 50 units to 0.096 at 800, a log-log slope of $-0.513$
against the $-0.500$ predicted. The second is irreducible: the swap argument of Lemma~1 bounds the
perturbation of the calibration set by twice the total-variation distance, and no amount of
calibration data removes it. The certificate is presented as a worst-case planning bound rather
than a sharp estimate.

Figure~4.}

\subsection*{Minor comment 3: how $L$ is fitted}
\begin{comment}
Section 3.4 describes regressing $d_{TV}$ on $\lVert\Delta\psi\rVert$ ``over source pairs,'' which
with three temperatures gives only three pairs, too few to fit a reliable slope. Section 5.3
clarifies that the fit actually uses multiple (pair, $\eta$) configurations. Move that clarification
into Section 3.4 itself so the estimation procedure is not misread as relying on three data points.
\end{comment}

\noindent\textbf{Response.} Clarified and moved to the method, where it belongs. The design points
are configurations rather than conditions: each ordered pair of source conditions contributes one
point per distinct departure magnitude the source data exhibits, so a fleet with three conditions
supplies far more than three points. In the study reported in Section~5.3 this gives 30
(pair, departure) configurations, of which 60\% are used for the fit and the remaining 40\% are held
out to validate the resulting bound.
